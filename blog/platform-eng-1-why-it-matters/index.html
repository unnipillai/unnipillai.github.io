The Problem No One Wants to Admit

Your developers are spending more time wiring pipelines than writing business logic, and in most cases the cause is the platform underneath them.

Across the digital-first organizations I’ve worked with in fintech, banking and telecom, I keep seeing the same pattern: smart engineers, good intentions, and a delivery model that is falling apart under the surface. People are working hard. The infrastructure under them was never designed to scale at the speed the business now demands.

1. What the Current State Actually Looks Like

Leave the architecture diagrams and vendor slides aside for a moment. This is what most engineering organizations deal with day to day:

ChallengeWhat It Looks Like
Siloed PipelinesInfrastructure and application delivery run as separate workflows, with different teams, different tools and unclear ownership. A change to infra needs a ticket. A change to an app goes through a different pipeline, and nothing ties the two together.
Manual Infra ProvisioningCloud and on-prem infrastructure is managed through tickets, scripts and ad hoc automation. The result is drift, delays, inconsistent compliance, and engineers waiting days for environments they should be able to spin up in minutes.
Lack of StandardizationEvery team builds its own CI/CD, monitoring and observability stack. Work gets duplicated, knowledge stays tribal instead of documented, and security gaps go unnoticed until an audit.
Operational OverheadDevelopers spend their time learning cloud-specific constructs, managing environments and handling ops tasks that have nothing to do with the product they’re building.

This operating model adds cognitive load, slows time to market and creates risk you could have avoided, which hurts most in regulated industries like financial services.

Drift, duplication, delays and developer fatigue are the current state for most organizations, and most of them underestimate what it is costing them.

2. What Platform Engineering Actually Is

A lot of organizations start platform engineering by picking a tool: “let’s adopt Backstage” or “let’s standardize on Terraform.” Those are implementation choices. Platform engineering is a strategic capability that changes how your whole engineering organization operates.

The philosophy underneath it is “you build it, you run it.”

Engineering teams own their services end to end, from code to production. They provision infrastructure themselves instead of filing tickets, deploy without waiting on a separate ops team, and run their own monitoring.

This is where most organizations get it wrong. You can’t declare “you build it, you run it” and walk away. Give developers full ownership without the tools, guardrails and automation to handle it, and they end up buried in operational work they were never set up to do.

Closing that gap is the platform’s job.

3. The Vision: A Developer Internal Platform

The goal is a code-first, API-driven internal developer platform that lets developers build, deploy and operate services on their own, safely, without having to understand the infrastructure underneath.

Through the platform, developers should be able to:

  • Scaffold a new service from golden templates, pre-wired with CI/CD, logging, tracing and compliance hooks
  • Provision infrastructure on demand (databases, queues, caches, storage) without learning cloud-specific APIs
  • Spin up full environments (dev, staging, test) from templates in minutes
  • Deploy through standardized pipelines with built-in security scanning, policy checks and automated rollbacks
  • Operate their services with observability, alerting and cost visibility already configured

What they should not have to do:

  • File tickets for infrastructure
  • Learn the specifics of AWS, Azure, or GCP services
  • Build their own CI/CD pipelines from scratch
  • Manually configure monitoring, logging, or compliance checks
  • Navigate a different workflow depending on which team they’re on

The platform hides the complexity and leaves developers in control. They get a clean, consistent experience while the platform team handles the hard parts underneath.

4. Guiding Principles

Five principles define how the platform operates:

PrincipleWhat It Means
Everything as CodeAll infrastructure, pipeline logic and policies are managed as versioned code, with no manual changes in consoles. That removes configuration drift, keeps everything auditable and reproducible, and puts every change through review.
Self-Service FirstDevelopers provision services, environments and infrastructure through the platform, without tickets, manual ops or waiting. Every capability is available through the API, the CLI and the UI.
Policy-Driven GovernanceCompliance, security and cost controls are built into the platform as policy-as-code. Because the guardrails are automated, developers can move fast inside safe boundaries.
Unified Developer ExperienceOne consistent interface across API, CLI and UI gives every persona the same low-friction workflow. A junior developer and a senior architect get the same experience.
Platform as ProductThe platform is run as an internal product, with developers as its customers. The platform team tracks adoption, satisfaction and velocity, and developer feedback drives the roadmap.

The most important design choice is API-first. Everything goes through the API layer, and the CLI and UI are consumers of that API rather than separate systems. That keeps behavior consistent: a developer using the CLI gets the same results and the same policy enforcement as one using the UI.

Part 2 goes through each layer in depth: what it does, who owns it, and how governance is built into it.

6. Where Most Organizations Get It Wrong

I see three failure patterns again and again.

They treat it as a tooling project

“We’ll adopt Backstage and call it platform engineering.” Tools matter, but the platform is the capability around them: the operating model, the self-service workflows, the governance automation, and the developer experience that ties it all together.

They build without treating developers as customers

The platform team builds what it thinks developers need, never asks for feedback, and then wonders why adoption is low. A platform nobody adopts is just infrastructure with a fancy name.

They skip governance until it’s too late

Self-service without guardrails is dangerous, especially in regulated industries. The organizations that get this right build compliance in from day one instead of bolting it on after the first audit finding.
